Topic vs. thesis (6:20). A topic is a broad subject area, a starting point: "Remote work."

A thesis is a specific, arguable claim, a promise about what you will prove: "Remote work reduces burnout when managers set clear boundaries."

What makes a strong thesis? (6:40) One clear sentence that tells the reader exactly what your text will explain or prove.

Specific: focused on one main idea. Supportable: can be backed by evidence. Clear: the reader knows what to expect.

5

Think-pair-share: build a thesis

Topic: "The impact of artificial intelligence on the job market"

7:05 · 15 min

Think (3 min, alone)

Pair (5 min)

Share (7 min, whole class)

Weak: "Artificial intelligence is changing jobs." Too vague; no specific direction and no claim to support.

Acceptable: "Artificial intelligence is making some jobs obsolete while creating new roles in the tech industry." Names two effects, but does not argue which is more significant.

Strong: "While artificial intelligence will eliminate many routine jobs, it will generate a net increase in employment by creating new roles in AI oversight, data analysis and human-centered services." Specific and arguable, with three supporting directions built in.

Types of supporting evidence

Every body paragraph makes one claim, then provides evidence to support it.

7:20 · 20 min

Facts and statistics
Verified data, numbers or documented facts.

Examples
Concrete cases that illustrate your point.

Reasoning
A logical explanation of why something is true.

Expert opinion
Quotations or paraphrases from credible authorities.

Model text: how professionals build a thesis

Read the text, then find the thesis and the evidence.

8:10 · 15 min

"The Case for Learning Languages," adapted from The Economist

In an era of machine translation and instant communication, one might reasonably question whether the effort required to learn a second language is worth it. The answer, supported by decades of neuroscientific research, is an emphatic yes, not simply for the practical advantages of bilingualism, but for the profound cognitive, social, and professional benefits that accompany the process of language acquisition itself.

Studies from the University of Edinburgh demonstrate that bilingual individuals develop stronger executive function, delaying the onset of dementia by an average of four years. Beyond the medical evidence, language learners consistently report greater empathy and cross-cultural understanding, skills increasingly valued by multinational employers.

In groups of three. Be ready to share your answers with the class.

1. Learning a second language is worth the effort because it brings cognitive, social and professional benefits beyond communication.

2. Three claims. Cognitive: bilingualism strengthens executive function and delays dementia. Social: learners develop empathy and cultural awareness. Professional: employers value these skills.

3. Cognitive: a research study (University of Edinburgh) with a statistic. Social: learners' reported experience. Professional: reasoning about what employers value.

4. Yes. The opening admits that machine translation exists, then answers it. This makes the argument feel fair and grounded.

5. The text would read as a list of disconnected facts. The thesis is the organizing idea: it tells the reader why the evidence matters.

TAG Peer Feedback Guide

Use with your partner's 250-word paragraph from Part 9.

9:20 · 20 min

TAG is a structured peer feedback method. It keeps your feedback balanced, specific and actionable. Follow the three steps in order.

TTELL

Tell your partner something specific that is working well.

Name WHAT is working and WHY it is effective.

  • "Your thesis is effective because it makes a specific claim about ___ and implies the structure you will use."
  • "The evidence in your second sentence is strong because ___"
  • "Your concluding sentence works well because ___"
AASK

Ask a genuine question about something you want to understand.

Ask about something unclear, surprising, or that you would like more detail on.

  • "I want to ask about your evidence in ___ — what is the source for that information?"
  • "I'm wondering why you chose ___ as your main claim — can you explain your thinking?"
  • "Your concluding sentence surprised me — could you tell me more about what you meant by ___?"
GGIVE

Give one specific, actionable suggestion for improvement.

Be constructive and precise. Focus on ONE thing — not everything at once.

  • "You might consider adding a transition between sentences 3 and 4 to show how your two pieces of evidence connect."
  • "Your thesis could be stronger if you made the claim about ___ more specific."
  • "The evidence in sentence ___ would be more convincing if you explained HOW it supports your thesis."

Remember. Good feedback is specific, honest and kind. It names the exact place in the text, explains why it works (or doesn't), and gives the writer something to act on. Vague feedback ("It was good") helps no one. Be the kind of reader you would want.
